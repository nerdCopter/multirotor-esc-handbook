I've always assumed that the legs or leads of a capacitor are made from nickel-plated copper wire, a solid copper core with a thin coating of nickel for corrosion resistance and to make it easy to solder, just like every other wire on our drones. But that couldn't be further from the truth. Most capacitor leads are made of copper-clad steel, a steel wire plated with copper and then plated with nickel over the top for corrosion resistance and solderability. At high currents, these steel leads can act like toaster wires and get super hot. The capacitors we solder to our ESCs are subjected to incredible abuse. At mid-throttle, we're switching power to the motor phases thousands of times every second, and every time those FETs switch on or off, there is a rush of current either into or out of the capacitor. All of that current has to flow through the capacitor leads, and that generates resistive heating via the I squared R rule. Obviously, the higher the resistance of the lead, the more heat is generated by the current flowing into or out of the capacitor. But, it's worse than that because that I squared R heating heats up the leg of the capacitor, and the higher the temperature, the higher the resistivity. So, it's quite easy to get a thermal runaway effect, whereby the resistivity climbs with temperature, which means that more heat is generated, which means that the temperature climbs even further, until the capacitor lead gets so hot that it melts and separates from the ESC. The moment that lead melts, the capacitor is no longer connected to the ESC, and that means there is nowhere for that surge of current to go. And so, it creates enormous voltage spikes, which is often enough to blow up the FETs, TVS diode, or voltage regulator on the ESC, and any of those failing will cause your drone to immediately fall out of the sky. So, now we understand the problem, but that doesn't explain why some capacitor legs get hot enough to melt steel, whilst others stay pretty cool even under the most extreme loads. The answer is that not all copper-clad steel is created equal. There are different grades with vastly different electrical properties. The resistivity of copper-clad steel is graded using the IACS, the International Annealed Copper Standard. This compares the resistance of a copper-clad steel wire to a pure copper wire of the same length and diameter. If the resistance is the same, that has an IACS rating of 100%. If, however, the copper-clad steel wire has twice the resistance of the copper wire, then that has an IACS rating of 50%. Standard cheap copper-clad steel wire has an IACS rating of about 20%. That makes it five times the resistance of a copper wire of the same length and diameter. But, it depends on grade. You can get higher-grade, more expensive copper-clad steel wire with IACS ratings of 30 or even 40%. The difference is just the thickness of the copper coating on the steel wire. The more copper you plate onto the steel, the lower the resistance of the copper-clad steel wire overall, and the higher the IACS grade. As you can imagine, cheaper unbranded capacitors, even if they're marked low ESR, are going to be using the cheaper grades of copper-clad steel with a resistance of 20% IACS. This resistance is simply too high for a drone application. We are pushing too much current through the leads. They are going to get super hot and potentially melt. Unfortunately, it's very difficult to determine what grade of copper-clad steel a particular manufacturer is using when they make a capacitor. Both 20% and 40% IACS respond similarly to a magnetic field. They have similar strength, similar stiffness. It really comes down just to the conductivity, which is very hard to measure for a capacitor lead that's very short and relatively conductive. Unless you happen to have a thermal camera pointed at the capacitor legs while you're driving a motor, it's really difficult to determine if you've got one of the good ones or if your capacitor is a ticking time bomb just waiting to make your drone fall out of the sky. But, the leads melting is not the only way that I have seen capacitors fail during this ESC testing. There are other much more catastrophic failure modes and we need to understand these in order to get a full picture of capacitor durability. We use two different types of capacitors on our ESCs, wet electrolytic and solid polymer. These two different types have very different constructions, performance, and durability. Wet electrolytic capacitors are constructed of an aluminum foil anode coated in a thin layer of aluminum oxide which acts as a dielectric that separates the positive and negative sides of the capacitor. The cathode of the capacitor is constructed of a paper soaked in a liquid electrolyte. And when we push current into or draw current out of the capacitor, that current has to flow as ions within the liquid and that has a significant ESR or equivalent series resistance. Those ions have to bump their way past the liquid molecules and that limits how fast current can flow into or out of a wet electrolytic capacitor. Low ESR electrolytic caps have specialized liquid electrolytes that have maximum ion motility so that the ions can move quickly through the liquid to allow them to charge and discharge quickly and to reduce their equivalent series resistance or ESR. If we have a capacitor with an ESR that's too high, even if the leads are nice and conductive, the current flowing into and out of the capacitor will heat it up significantly over time. And if the capacitor gets too hot, that liquid electrolyte is going to boil and cause the capacitor to explode. Solid polymer capacitors have a very different construction. They share the same aluminum foil anode and aluminum oxide dielectric as wet electrolytic capacitors, but the cathode is composed of a solid polymer that is polymerized and grown onto the foil during manufacturing. This solid polymer carries electric charge into and out of the capacitor as electrons jumping along conductive polymer chains. And electrons moving through a solid in this way is enormously easier and more efficient than ions bumping through a liquid. The result is that a solid polymer capacitor can have an ESR of 1,000 to 10,000 times lower than a wet electrolytic capacitor. This lower equivalent series resistance means that we can push much more current into and out of a solid polymer capacitor before it starts to significantly heat up internally. Solid polymer capacitors have another advantage, and that is that they maintain their capacitance at high frequencies much better than wet electrolytic capacitors. This is because the aluminum foil inside the capacitor is etched with microscopic pits and tunnels to increase its surface area. In a wet electrolytic capacitor, the ions have a very hard time bumping their way through the liquid and getting into those tunnels and pits to access all of that surface area. It takes a while for the ions to move down those tunnels. At low frequencies, that's fine. The ions have plenty of time to get down the tunnels and access the whole surface area of the foil. But at high frequencies, they simply don't have time to move that distance. And so the ions only access the surface of the foil. They don't get deep into those tunnels and pits. That means that a wet electrolytic capacitor can lose anywhere from 20% to 50% of its effective capacitance at 48 kilohertz, for example. Normally, a capacitor is measured at about 120 hertz, but we are switching these capacitors at 24 kilohertz or 48 kilohertz. And that is a much, much higher frequency, and so we see a significant loss of capacitance with a wet electrolytic type. Because solid polymer capacitors have a much more conductive cathode, the electrons can zip down those polymer chains into the tunnels and the pits really, really quickly and access all of that surface area even at high frequency. This means that a solid polymer capacitor maintains its capacitance much, much better. And that means under use in an ESC, a 470 microfarad solid polymer capacitor might have more effective capacitance than a 1,000 microfarad wet electrolytic. Does this mean that we should all switch to using solid polymer capacitors for our future drone builds? Well, not necessarily because there is one final trade-off that you need to be aware of when comparing solid polymer and wet electrolytic. And that is durability to voltage spikes. When either of these types of capacitor is exposed to a voltage spike above its maximum voltage rating, it can cause a localized breakdown of that thin aluminum oxide layer between the anode and the cathode. As that aluminum oxide layer breaks down, it allows current to flow from the anode to the cathode and actually shorts out the capacitor. In a wet electrolytic type capacitor, the electrolyte reacts with the exposed aluminum layer to regrow that aluminum oxide and heal the damage from the spike. Provided that the damage is not too severe, the capacitor can continue operating with very, very little loss of performance. If, however, the damage is more severe in a wet electrolytic capacitor, the heat generated from the current flowing from the anode to the cathode will boil away the electrolyte and effectively render the capacitor an open circuit. In a solid polymer capacitor, there is no electrolyte to react with the exposed aluminum layer, so that aluminum oxide dielectric cannot regrow. That means that a solid polymer capacitor cannot self-heal. And worse than that, if current starts flowing from the anode to the cathode, the heat generated can melt the solid polymer. It actually continues to conduct electricity and the whole capacitor will fail, short circuit, get incredibly hot, and explode. This means that solid polymer capacitors are little bit more fragile at the same maximum voltage rating compared to wet electrolytics. This brings us to the recommendations.
